The current production corpus has 157 records, but its composition is 52 Harvard Dataverse records, 50 DataCite records, 22 Pennsylvania catalog records, 15 curated registry records, and only 14 records in the live-validated national federal backbone.  More importantly, there is presently only one published state overlay. Thirty-four jurisdictions are still effectively navigation-only, eight are bounded evidence gaps, and several others are candidate/partial states rather than integrated catalogs.  Until that changes, I would make the UI extraordinarily explicit that USHSO currently provides a national federal baseline + selected state coverage, rather than letting “51 jurisdictions recognized” feel like 51 jurisdictions comprehensively indexed. The state/federal matrix work is therefore not ancillary to the website; it is probably the single largest improvement to the product.  



Retrieval quality is the biggest functional problem after coverage. Your frozen 60-question benchmark reports must-not-miss recall of only 0.50 at rank 10 and 0.548 at rank 20, while acceptable-source coverage is about 0.46 at 10 and 0.53 at 20.  Some individual benchmark questions return many irrelevant sources before obvious relevant ones; one example does not retrieve either acceptable source until deep in the result set.  For a researcher, this is more damaging than a missing animation, filter, or polished card. I would invest heavily in intent decomposition, source-class priors, synonym/construct expansion, negative constraints, authoritative-source boosting, and explicit near-miss detection. The goal should be: the first 3–5 results are defensible research choices, not merely records with lexical overlap.  Stop returning only “datasets”; return a research evidence plan. Suppose I ask: “I want to study whether hospital acquisitions in Texas were followed by changes in utilization and financial performance.” I don't really want ten independent search results. I want USHSO to say, in effect: “You probably need an ownership/change source, a facility financial source, a utilization source, and potentially an identity crosswalk. Here are the best available sources for each role; here is what can and cannot currently be linked.” This is where USHSO could become dramatically more useful than Google Dataset Search, Data.gov, or a generic catalog. You already have the ingredients—controlled concepts, access metadata, join objects, evidence states.  What is missing is the research-workflow synthesis layer.  Entity resolution is eventually unavoidable. This becomes obvious almost immediately in health-systems research. A “hospital,” CMS-certified facility, legal entity, parent system, campus, NPI organization, EIN filer, ownership record, and state-licensed facility are not interchangeable. Your architecture explicitly avoids autonomous identity merging today, which is appropriately cautious.  But as a researcher, I need USHSO eventually to tell me: “These two datasets refer to facilities using CCN; this one uses NPI; this finance source represents the Medicare-certified entity rather than the consolidated health system; this ownership relationship is candidate-only.” I would not build one magical canonical identity table prematurely. I would build an identity graph with typed assertions, source-specific identifiers, confidence, effective dates, and unresolved conflicts. That will become one of the Observatory's most valuable assets. Add “Can I actually use this for my question?” metadata. The details page has excellent factual metadata, but I still want a researcher-oriented summary near the top: Best for, Not sufficient for, Key analytic cautions, Typical unit of observation, Known breaks in series, Update frequency/lag, suppression/completeness, and identifier stability over time. For access-controlled sources, add practical information when known: request process, approximate turnaround category, fee basis, DUA/application requirements, delivery format, and who qualifies. Currently you expose many of the raw ingredients, but the researcher has to synthesize their implications.  Make search results much lighter and more decision-oriented. The current card implementation still has rank, family status, relevance, relationship, verification information, seven metadata values, a field summary, access routes, and multiple actions.  That is too much before I've decided whether the source is worth inspecting. I would make a result primarily: title → one-sentence description → why it matches → geography/grain/time → access/verification → View details. Fortunately, your current draft PR #2 is already moving almost exactly in that direction and also fixes misleading bounded result counts.  I would finish that work rather than redesigning the page again.  Fix ordinary web discoverability. Ironically, USHSO is now fairly machine-friendly through /api/*, /llms.txt, CORS and WebMCP, but less friendly to conventional crawlers. The base HTML is essentially an empty React root with a generic title/description.  The Worker-generated sitemap currently lists top-level pages but not the stable dataset detail pages.  That is why my web crawler could reach ushso.org but could not actually read the application's content. I would prerender/server-render every stable dataset page, include every record in the sitemap, and publish schema.org/Dataset/DCAT-style structured metadata, canonical URLs, and record-specific titles/descriptions. Then a search for a specific obscure healthcare dataset could land directly on the USHSO explanation of it.  As a researcher today, I would absolutely use USHSO for source reconnaissance and understanding unfamiliar datasets. I would not yet trust it for exhaustive source identification or conclude that its top results constitute the best available evidence.
